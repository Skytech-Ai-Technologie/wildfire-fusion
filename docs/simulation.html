The story: a fire lookout that never sleeps

Imagine a watchtower guarding a forest. A human watcher gets tired, misses things at night, and panics or gets bored. We want a machine watcher instead. The machine gets information from:

• a camera that sees normal colors (like your eyes — good in daylight)
• a camera that sees heat (called LWIR or thermal — works at night and through light smoke)
• cheap weather sensors on the ground that measure heat, humidity, smoke particles and gases (called IIoT — “Industrial Internet of Things”)

The goal: shout “FIRE!” as early as possible — but without crying wolf. A false alarm costs money and trust; a missed alarm costs the forest. The whole project is about balancing those two mistakes.

A smoke detector at home beeps when one sensor triggers. In a forest, one trigger is not enough — wind dust looks like smoke, a hot rock looks like fire. That is why we need several “eyes” and a smart way to combine them.

Three experts work together

The system is a small team of three “experts”, each checking the other's work:

1 · The Eyes (perception) Two neural networks look at the two cameras, plus a simpler “sniffer” that scores the weather sensors. Each expert says: “I am 62% sure I see something.” The heat camera and the color camera each have their own attention module (called CBAM) so they focus on the important part of the picture.
2 · The Brain (context) Confidence numbers jump around: one blurry frame can scare the Eyes. The Brain looks at the last 32 moments as a story and asks: is this confidence growing like a real fire, or just noise? Uncertain experts get turned down (this is the “entropy gate”) before they can mislead anyone.
3 · The Judge (decision) The Judge combines all opinions using a mathematical voting rule (Dempster–Shafer). Clever part: when two experts strongly disagree, instead of averaging blindly it becomes extra cautious. Only when the combined belief passes a threshold τ does the alarm actually fire — and even then, it must repeat on the next tick before it is confirmed.

After the Judge, a “duty officer” program applies the safety rules: require the alarm twice in a row, latch it until a human acknowledges, then cool down before re-arming. The alarm travels as a tiny message (< 8 kB) over a radio-style channel (MQTT) to the firefighters' system.

Watch the simulation — the system thinking out loud

You do not need any dataset or hardware to see this work. The project ships a simulation that replays a fire day minute by minute: three fake sensors slowly notice a fire, the three experts process every tick, and the alarm fires at the moment the evidence becomes strong. The output is a timeline you can open in any spreadsheet:

python scripts\run_field_sim.py --events 3 --config plus_iiot

Two files appear in results\field_sim\: a timeline (every tick, every confidence, every decision) and a summary. One summary number to love: the detection horizon — how many seconds before a plain camera would have shouted, our combined system already shouted. Negative = earlier = better.

Think of the simulation as a flight simulator for the alarm system: we can test the “duty officer” rules — alarms, silences, lost sensors — safely, thousands of times, before trusting real hardware.

There are four configurations to compare: camera only, camera + heat, camera + weather sensors, and weather sensors alone. Running all four shows why each sensor is worth having — that comparison is one of the project's tables.

What we contribute — the eight things, in plain words

The architecture (the “three experts” idea) comes from a research paper. Our scientific contribution is making it real, correct, testable and honest — here is exactly what we added.

1We built the whole machine — and wrote down every guess. The paper skips details (how to train the Brain, how deep to fuse the cameras, what to do when a sensor dies). We filled each gap and labeled it openly as an assumption, so anyone can swap in better values later.
2We made the exam fair (no cheating). Neural networks are graded on photos they have never seen. We discovered that the standard way of splitting D-Fire puts nearly identical video frames in both the study set and the exam — like seeing exam questions the night before. The score looks great but is a lie. We regrouped the photos so the exam is honest.
3We grade with error bars. Instead of saying “73%”, we say “73%, and with high confidence it is between 71% and 75%”. We also count false alarms against an openly defined denominator and save every raw timing measurement, so nothing is hidden.
4We counted everything before trusting it. The D-Fire dataset is famous for being labeled inconsistently (is 0 “smoke” or “fire”?). Our download script writes a provenance receipt and automatically checks the label counts against the published numbers — they matched exactly.
5We wrote the duty-officer safety rules. The paper never says when an alarm may repeat, or how long to wait after a human clears it. We specified those rules, tested them, and even found and fixed a subtle hole (an alarm sneaking in during “cooldown” could re-arm itself with a single tick).
6The Judge calibrates itself — and refuses nonsense. A script searches for the best voting settings on real validation data. If you accidentally feed it data without labels, it refuses to run instead of quietly producing garbage that would later override the safety threshold.
7It is built for small hardware. The models export to ONNX (a portable format) and are compressed to 8-bit integers for Jetson-style devices. Alarm messages are hard-capped at 8 kB — small enough for weak forest radio links.
8Everything is tested, twice. 55 automated tests guard the tricky math and the safety rules, and this very page has a big brother: the interactive explainer, where you can drag sliders and watch the fusion math react, completely offline.

Try it yourself — three commands, no data needed

:: 1. watch the alarm system think (5 seconds)
python scripts\run_field_sim.py --events 3 --config plus_iiot

:: 2. run all 55 safety and math tests
pytest -v

:: 3. open the interactive explainer (double-click)
docs\index.html

Want the real thing? The main page shows how to download the datasets and train the models — that part needs a GPU (a few hours) or patience on a laptop.
